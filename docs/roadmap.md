---

## 0. Design principles

1. **Every stage ends with a runnable artifact and an exit test.** You don't advance until the test passes. No half-working stages stacked on each other.
2. **Free data and free tools only.** No lab access needed until a mentor exists.
3. **One flagship project, plus a "ladder" of small wins.** Small simulations validate your skills and become figures in the final paper and in the email to a professor.
4. **Validate against known answers.** Every simulation is checked against an analytic result or a published number before you trust it.
5. **Negative results count.** Log them.

## Phase 1: Foundations and first working simulations (Weeks 2–5)

Each stage pairs **one concept + one derivation + one simulation + one validation.** This is the Step 1–7 method in your myplan.md.

**Stage 1.1: Crystals and the periodic potential.**
- *Learn:* lattices, unit cells, Bravais lattices, reciprocal lattice, Bloch's theorem (intuition first).
- *Build:* `src/lattice.py` generates 1D/2D lattices and reciprocal vectors. Load a real structure (e.g., silicon or graphene CIF from the Crystallography Open Database) with `pymatgen`.
- *Exit test:* your computed silicon lattice constant, reciprocal vectors and nearest-neighbor distance match the CIF and pymatgen to 4 digits.

**Stage 1.2: Tight-binding band structure (the core skill).**
- *Learn:* the 1D chain E(k) = −2t cos(ka), then a two-atom basis (SSH model or a polyatomic chain) showing a gap, then 2D square and honeycomb lattices.
- *Math:* only matrix eigenvalues and complex exponentials. You have this.
- *Build:* `src/tb.py` builds H(k), diagonalizes it over a k-path, and plots the bands.
- *Exit test (analytic):* 1D chain matches −2t cos(ka) to machine precision. The honeycomb lattice shows Dirac points at K with E=0 and linear dispersion near K (graphene).
- *Exit test (published):* the nearest-neighbor graphene hopping t≈2.7–3 eV gives a Fermi velocity near 10⁶ m/s. Compare to literature values and cite them.

**Stage 1.3: Statistical mechanics and the Ising model.**
- *Learn:* Boltzmann distribution, partition function, order parameters, phase transitions, Monte Carlo (Metropolis).
- *Build:* `src/ising.py`, 2D Ising Metropolis, with magnetization, energy, specific heat and susceptibility vs T.
- *Exit test:* T_c ≈ 2.269 J/k_B (Onsager). Your peak in specific heat and susceptibility must land within a few % for L=32–64. Also show finite-size scaling (peak sharpening with L).
- This gives you the vocabulary of phase transitions that superconductivity shares.

**Stage 1.4: Reading real papers.**
- Read 3 papers (see Reading list). For each, write a one-page note: question, method, result, limitation, one follow-up idea.
- *Exit test:* you can explain each paper's main figure aloud in 2 minutes without notes.
- Use Google Scholar, arXiv, and Semantic Scholar to find *recent* (2024–2026) follow-ups. This is your "new research" intake. Save each with a link in `docs/papers.md`.

**Stage 1.5: Superconductivity concepts (no heavy math yet).**
- *Learn:* zero resistance, the Meissner effect, Cooper pairs, BCS gap Δ ≈ 1.76 k_B T_c, Type I vs II, conventional vs cuprate and nickelate and hydride.
- *Build:* plot the BCS gap vs T using the numerical gap equation (a 1D root-find per temperature).
- *Exit test:* Δ(0)/k_BT_c = 1.76 and Δ→0 at T_c with the right square-root shape.

**Phase 1 gate:** Stages 1.1–1.3 and 1.5 pass their tests, and you've written 3 paper notes. If you fall behind, drop 1.5's math, never the tests.

## Phase 2: Free data pipeline (Weeks 4–8)

You are learning to get real materials data, clean it, and ask whether it's trustworthy.

### Free data sources

| Source | What it has | Access |
|---|---|---|
| **Materials Project** | DFT band gaps, formation energies, structures, many properties | Free API key, `mp-api` / pymatgen |
| **JARVIS-DFT (NIST)** | DFT data incl. 2D materials, superconductors (computed Tc), optical properties | Free, `jarvis-tools`, downloadable JSON |
| **OQMD, AFLOW** | Large DFT databases | Free REST APIs |
| **NOMAD** | Raw and processed computations, large archive | Free |
| **C2DB / 2DMatPedia** | 2D material properties | Free downloads |
| **SuperCon (NIMS)** | Experimental superconductor Tc records | Free, registration; check terms |
| **3DSC dataset** (Sommer et al., *Scientific Data*, 2023) | SuperCon records matched to 3D structures | Free |
| **Crystallography Open Database** | Experimental crystal structures | Free, CIF |
| **Matbench / matminer datasets** | Curated ML benchmark tasks | Free |

**Verify each license and citation requirement** before use, and cite every source in your paper.

**Stage 2.1: First API pull.**
Fetch all binary semiconductors with a band gap listed from Materials Project (or JARVIS). Save raw JSON to `data/raw/`.
*Exit test:* one script, `python src/data.py`, regenerates the exact dataset from scratch, and records the date and database version.

**Stage 2.2: Sanity checks on data.**
Plot a band-gap histogram. Check for missing values, duplicates, and known-wrong cases (e.g., DFT underestimates gaps: silicon ~0.6 eV in PBE vs ~1.1 eV experiment).
*Exit test:* a short written note quantifying the DFT-vs-experiment gap for 10+ materials. This teaches you about **systematic error**, which impresses judges and professors.

**Stage 2.3: Tight-binding meets real data.**
Take a 2D material or semiconductor from the database, extract its band gap, and fit a simple 2-band tight-binding model to its reported gap and effective mass.
*Exit test:* the fit reproduces target gap to within 5%, and you state which physics the simple model leaves out.

**Stage 2.4: First ML baseline (honest version).**
Predict band gap (or Tc) from composition features using `matminer`/`scikit-learn` (random forest or gradient boosting).
*Exit test:* baseline MAE reported against a trivial baseline (predict the mean). A model that doesn't beat the dumb baseline is a finding, not a failure.

**Phase 2 gate:** one command regenerates data, features, baseline model and figures end to end.

## Phase 3: Flagship project (Weeks 7–13)

### Recommended direction

> **"How well do superconductor Tc predictors generalize to unseen chemical families?"**
> A rigorous, leakage-aware evaluation of data-driven Tc prediction on SuperCon/3DSC.

**Why this fits you:**
- Needs only Python and free data, so it can start immediately and fits 5 hrs/week.
- Superconductivity is your stated interest and connects to real physics (BCS, cuprates, hydrides).
- The scientific value is in **evaluation methodology**, not a flashy new model. That's achievable at your level and genuinely useful: many published Tc models use random train/test splits, and compounds in the same family (e.g., cuprates) leak across splits and inflate accuracy.
- The question is clear, falsifiable and measurable.

**Literature check (searched 2026-11-14): the "novel" part is weaker than I first said.** Leave-one-cluster-out (LOCO) cross-validation for superconductor Tc was introduced by Meredig et al., *Can machine learning identify the next high-temperature superconductor?* (Mol. Syst. Des. Eng., 2018), which showed random splits overestimate discovery performance. The 3DSC dataset (Sommer et al., Sci. Data 2023, arXiv:2212.06071) adds crystal structures. I found no paper combining LOCO-style evaluation with 3DSC's structure features, but that was one quick search, not a proof. So position the project as a **replication-and-extension** (does the Meredig finding hold on a newer, structure-aware dataset? do structure features help on held-out families?), not as a new idea. Also see Lee, arXiv:2406.14524 (2024) on out-of-domain Tc prediction.

**Honest novelty warning.** ML Tc prediction is an active, crowded area, and "leave-one-cluster-out" evaluation exists in the broader materials-ML literature. Your Stage 3.1 literature review decides whether your *specific* angle is new. If it isn't, narrow it (e.g., a specific family, or physics-informed features such as electron count and Debye temperature proxies) rather than claim novelty you can't support. Do not claim "first" without evidence.

### Backup flagships (switch at the Stage 3.1 checkpoint if the literature kills the main one)

| Option | Idea | Tradeoff |
|---|---|---|
| B | Systematic study of how tight-binding hopping range and lattice distortion open gaps in 2D lattices (kagome, Lieb, honeycomb) | More theory and math; very safe feasibility; originality in the parameter sweep |
| C | Database mining: which 2D materials in C2DB/JARVIS have flat bands near the Fermi level (candidates for correlated physics) | Strong research hook; needs careful band-structure handling |
| D | Monte Carlo study of finite-size and disorder effects on an Ising/XY-type system | Easiest; lowest novelty |

### Stages

**Stage 3.1: Literature review and gap (Weeks 7–8).**
Read ~10 papers on ML Tc prediction and dataset splitting. Build a table: dataset, features, split method, reported error. Search 2024–2026 for recent work (arXiv cond-mat.supr-con and cs.LG, Google Scholar "cited by" on 3DSC).
*Exit test:* a one-page gap statement, "Prior work did X; nobody (that I found) did Y; I will test Y," reviewed by a teacher or mentor. If Y exists, pivot to a backup.

**Stage 3.2: Dataset curation (Week 8).**
Build a cleaned dataset: parse formulas, remove duplicates, flag non-superconductors, handle conflicting Tc records, assign each compound a **chemical family label** (cuprate, iron-based, hydride, conventional elemental/alloy, etc.) using documented rules.
*Exit test:* a data card (source, version, size, cleaning rules, known biases). Spot-check 30 random rows by hand.

**Stage 3.3: Models and splits (Weeks 9–10).**
Train 2–3 model types. Evaluate with (a) random k-fold, (b) leave-one-family-out, (c) grouped-by-element splits.
*Exit test:* every number comes from a seeded script with unit tests confirming no row appears in both train and test, and that the family splits really exclude the held-out family.

**Stage 3.4: Analysis (Weeks 11–12).**
Quantify the gap between random and family-held-out error, with bootstrap confidence intervals. Examine which families fail and *why*, using physics: is the failure about mechanism (phonon vs. unconventional), chemistry, or data scarcity?
*Exit test:* each claim has a figure, an uncertainty, and a stated limitation. Include controls, such as shuffled-label models that should perform at chance.

**Stage 3.5: Robustness and replication (Week 13).**
Re-run on a second source (e.g., JARVIS or an alternative curated dataset) or with different random seeds.
*Exit test:* a fresh clone of the repo plus `make all` (or one documented script) regenerates every figure. Have a friend try it.

**Phase 3 gate:** results reproduce from scratch, the control behaves as expected, and you can state the conclusion in one sentence with a number and an error bar.

*Exit test:* a table of 5 candidates: group, topic, a paper you read, a specific connection to your project, and their stated policy on outside students.

**Stage 4.2: Draft the email (Week 9–10).**
Under ~200 words. Include: who you are, one sentence on a specific paper of theirs and a *specific* question about it, your concrete result so far (link to a one-page summary and GitHub), and a modest ask (a 15-minute conversation, or advice on a next step), not "can I join your lab."
*Exit test:* a teacher or parent reviews it. No generic text.

**Stage 4.3: Send, then follow up once.**
Send to 3–5 people individually (never mass-CC). Follow up once after ~10 days. Silence is common, so don't read it as rejection.
Also pursue **parallel routes**: your school's science teacher, UW outreach programs for high schoolers, local community research programs, and grad students or postdocs who may respond more readily. Check which of these are currently open and their eligibility.

**Stage 4.4: If a mentor engages.**
Ask what they'd find useful, and adapt your project to a question they care about. Never present mentor-contributed work as solely yours. Document exactly what you did vs. what they did (competitions require this).

**Stage 5.4: Release.**
Public GitHub repo with README, data card, environment file and a tagged release. Consider a preprint only after mentor and teacher review. Don't promise publication, and note that arXiv has its own endorsement requirements.
